Context, Position & Retrieval

The Five Papers

Retrieval and context handling are where a raw language model becomes a useful, grounded system. These five publications cover the two halves of the problem: how a model represents position inside its context window, and how it pulls in external knowledge to answer beyond its parameters.

  • RoFormer — Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE)
  • Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)
  • Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR)
  • Lost in the Middle — How LLMs Use Long Contexts

01

RoFormer: Enhanced Transformer with Rotary Position Embedding

Su, Lu, Pan, Murtadha, Wen & Liu · 2021 2021 · arXiv:2104.09864

The problem

The original Transformer added absolute sinusoidal position embeddings to token embeddings, and later work bolted on learned relative-position biases. Both are awkward: absolute embeddings don't naturally express the relative distance between two tokens that attention actually cares about, and they generalise poorly to sequence lengths unseen during training.

The contribution

RoPE encodes absolute position by rotating each query and key vector by an angle proportional to its position. Because the dot product of two rotated vectors depends only on the difference of their rotation angles, attention scores become a function of the relative distance between positions — with no extra parameters. The rotation gives a clean long-range decay and extrapolates more gracefully to longer sequences.

Why it matters to a practising engineer

RoPE is now the default position encoding in Llama, Mistral, Qwen, Gemma and most modern open-weights models. Understanding it is essential for context-length extension: tricks like NTK-aware scaling, linear position interpolation and YaRN all work by reshaping RoPE's rotation frequencies. When you extend a model's context window, you are almost always manipulating RoPE.

Engineer's takeaway

If you ever change rope_theta or apply a scaling factor in a model config, you are directly editing the rotation frequencies described in this paper. Get it wrong and the model's attention to relative position breaks — coherence collapses past the original training length.

02

Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks

Lewis, Perez, Piktus, Petroni, Karpukhin, Goyal, Küttler et al. (Meta / FAIR) · 2020 2020 · arXiv:2005.11401

The problem

A purely parametric model stores all of its knowledge in its weights. That knowledge is frozen at training time, hard to update, hard to attribute to a source, and prone to confident hallucination. For knowledge-intensive tasks — open-domain QA, fact verification — a model that cannot cite where an answer came from is fundamentally limited.

The contribution

RAG couples a parametric seq2seq generator (BART) with a non-parametric retriever (DPR over a dense Wikipedia index). For a query, the retriever fetches the top-k passages; those passages are prepended to the input so generation is conditioned on retrieved evidence. The result: grounding in real sources, knowledge you can update by swapping the index, and markedly fewer hallucinations — all trained end-to-end.

Why it matters to a practising engineer

This is the founding paper of the pattern that now underpins most production LLM applications. The two design choices it introduced — RAG-Sequence (one passage conditions the whole output) vs RAG-Token (the model can attend to different passages per token) — still frame how we think about fusing retrieved evidence. Every chatbot grounded in a knowledge base is a descendant of this work.

Engineer's takeaway

RAG turns "retrain the model" into "re-index the corpus." Updatable knowledge and source attribution are not nice-to-haves bolted on later — they are the core reason the architecture exists. Treat the index as a first-class, versioned artefact.

03

Dense Passage Retrieval for Open-Domain Question Answering

Karpukhin, Oğuz, Min, Lewis, Wu, Edunov, Chen & Yih (Meta / FAIR) · 2020 2020 · arXiv:2004.04906

The problem

For open-domain QA, retrieval had long been dominated by sparse lexical methods like BM25 — powerful but brittle: they match on term overlap, so a question and its answer passage that use different vocabulary are invisible to each other. The open question was whether learned dense representations could actually beat a strong BM25 baseline in practice.

The contribution

DPR trains a dual-encoder: one BERT encoder for questions, one for passages. Training uses in-batch negatives — every other passage in the batch serves as a negative example, making each gradient step cheaply informative. At inference, retrieval is a simple inner product (maximum inner-product search) between the question vector and a pre-computed passage index. DPR beat BM25 by a wide margin on retrieval accuracy and became the retrieval backbone behind RAG.

Why it matters to a practising engineer

DPR is the direct ancestor of every modern embedding-based retriever (BGE, E5, GTE, OpenAI embeddings). The dual-encoder + ANN-index + inner-product pattern is the standard RAG retrieval stack. Two lessons carry forward: in-batch negatives are how contrastive retrievers are trained efficiently, and hard negatives (here, BM25-mined distractors) sharply improve quality.

Engineer's takeaway

When you pick an embedding model and a vector store, you are choosing a DPR-style dual encoder and a MIPS index. Knowing this paper tells you exactly which knobs — negative sampling, encoder choice, index precision — drive retrieval recall.

04

Lost in the Middle: How Language Models Use Long Contexts

Liu, Lin, Hewitt, Paranjape, Bevilacqua, Petroni & Liang (Stanford) · 2023 2023 · arXiv:2307.03172

The problem

Vendors race to advertise ever-larger context windows — 32k, 128k, 1M tokens. But a big context window is only useful if the model actually uses all of it. Does a model attend uniformly across its input, or does position matter?

The contribution

Through controlled multi-document QA experiments, the authors show a striking U-shaped curve: models retrieve a relevant fact most reliably when it sits at the very start or very end of the context, and accuracy sags in the middle. The effect is large and robust across models — a long context window does not imply uniform use of context.

Why it matters to a practising engineer

This is a direct design constraint on RAG and prompt construction. Where you place a retrieved passage in the prompt changes whether the model sees it. Practical consequences: re-rank so the most relevant chunks land at the start and end; don't blindly stuff 100 passages into a 128k window and assume the model reads them all; and treat "we have a long context model" as no substitute for good retrieval and re-ranking.

Engineer's takeaway

Position is a retrieval problem in disguise. Order your context deliberately — key evidence at the edges, filler in the middle — and keep the relevant set small. More tokens is not more attention.

05

Self-RAG: Learning to Retrieve, Generate, and Critique through Self-Reflection

Asai, Wu, Wang, Sil & Hajishirzi (University of Washington / Allen Institute for AI) · 2023 2023 · arXiv:2310.11511

The problem

Standard RAG retrieves a fixed number of passages for every query, whether or not retrieval is needed, and never checks whether the retrieved passages are actually relevant or whether its own output is supported by them. This wastes effort on simple queries and lets irrelevant passages drag generation off course.

The contribution

Self-RAG trains a single model to emit special reflection tokens that make retrieval and quality decisions on demand. The model decides whether to retrieve at all, critiques whether each retrieved passage is relevant, and critiques whether its own generated answer is supported by the evidence and useful. The result is adaptive, self-evaluating RAG that retrieves only when helpful and can filter its own mistakes.

Why it matters to a practising engineer

Self-RAG is the bridge from static retrieval pipelines to agentic, self-correcting RAG. The ideas — conditional retrieval, relevance grading of retrieved chunks, and self-verification of grounding — reappear directly in modern patterns like Corrective RAG (CRAG), retrieval graders in LangGraph, and reflection loops in agent frameworks. It reframes retrieval as a decision the model makes, not a fixed preprocessing step.

Engineer's takeaway

If you are building a RAG agent, the relevance-grading and groundedness-checking steps you write are exactly Self-RAG's reflection tokens, externalised as orchestration. Conditional retrieval saves cost; self-critique cuts hallucination.

06

What to Take Away

  • RoPE is the position encoding you actually ship. Relative position via rotation, no extra parameters, and the lever behind every context-extension trick (interpolation, NTK, YaRN).
  • RAG is the founding grounding pattern. Retrieve, then condition generation on evidence — updatable knowledge and attribution instead of frozen, unverifiable weights.
  • DPR is the retrieval backbone. Dual-encoder + in-batch negatives + inner-product search is the template for every modern embedding retriever.
  • Lost in the Middle is a hard constraint. A long context window is not uniformly used — order matters, so re-rank and keep the relevant set small.
  • Self-RAG makes retrieval a decision. Conditional retrieval plus self-critique is the seed of agentic, self-correcting RAG.

Together these five trace a single arc: from how a model represents position in its window, to how it grounds answers in external knowledge, to how it reasons about whether that knowledge is even worth retrieving.
